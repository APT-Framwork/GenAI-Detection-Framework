\chapter*{General Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{General Introduction}

The field of cybersecurity has never been more critical --- or more crowded
with literature. A quick search returns hundreds of papers, surveys, and
reviews, and yet something feels off. They all read the same. Same structure,
same detached tone, same walls of definitions delivered cold, as if the goal
is to exhaust the reader rather than enlighten them. It is as if the rise of
AI has infected academic writing itself: reports that feel generated for a
machine, not written for a human.

This state-of-the-art review is different. Not because the topic is different
--- it is, in fact, one of the most pressing challenges in modern computing:
the detection of \textbf{Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs)} using Machine
Learning. But because the \textit{way} we chose to approach it is different.
This is not the orthodox way of doing things. We did not set out to pump your
mind with random information and call it a survey. We set out to talk
\textit{to} you --- the reader --- whether you are a seasoned security
researcher or someone just stepping into this field for the first time. We use
analogies. We build intuition before we build formulas. We treat concepts like
conversations, not mandates.

\section*{What This Report Covers}

The report is structured around three interconnected pillars:
\textbf{APT attack modeling}, \textbf{threat detection frameworks}, and
\textbf{Machine Learning-based countermeasures}. It opens by establishing the
conceptual foundations --- what cybersecurity really means, what makes an APT
different from a regular cyberattack. Think of a burglar who quietly copies
your house key and comes back over months, stealing small things one at a
time, never triggering the alarm --- that is the essence of an APT, as opposed
to someone who smashes the door in and runs. The report then walks through
the frameworks that security professionals use to understand and map attacks
--- the \textbf{Cyber Kill Chain (CKC)} and the \textbf{MITRE ATT\&CK}
framework --- explaining not just what they are, but why they exist and how
they complement each other.

From there, the report moves into the present threat landscape of
2024--2025, where state-sponsored APT groups and AI-powered attack tools are
converging in ways that fundamentally challenge traditional defenses. It
examines how Generative AI is being weaponized at every stage of the kill
chain --- from automated reconnaissance to polymorphic malware --- while
simultaneously offering defenders powerful new capabilities.

\section*{The Heart of the Review}

The final chapter dives into the actual detection solutions: an honest,
critical study of the datasets used in APT research, the Machine Learning
algorithms most suited to each type of detection task, and a detailed review
of state-of-the-art ML, Deep Learning, and Generative AI approaches applied
to real APT datasets. Performance tables, architectural breakdowns, and honest
assessments of each method's strengths and limits are all included --- not to
overwhelm, but to give you a clear picture of where the field stands today and
where it is headed.


As AI continues to reshape how knowledge is produced and consumed, most
reports in this space have started to look and feel the same --- written by
a template, for a template, as if every survey was cast from the same mold.
We tried our best to break out of that shell. The goal was never to write
something that sounds impressive. The goal was to write something that is
actually useful --- something a real person can read, understand, and build
upon.

\chapter*{General Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{General Conclusion}

This report set out with a clear ambition: not simply to list what exists in
the literature, but to build a coherent, honest, and human-readable picture
of where the field of Machine Learning-based APT detection stands today ---
and where it is going.

\section*{Laying the Foundations}

Before any algorithm could be discussed, it was necessary to understand what
we are actually defending against. Advanced Persistent Threats are not
ordinary attacks. They are slow, deliberate, and adaptive campaigns --- often
state-sponsored --- that blend into the noise of normal network activity for
weeks or months before their damage becomes visible. Understanding their
nature, their phases, and the frameworks built to model them --- the
\textbf{Cyber Kill Chain (CKC)} and \textbf{MITRE ATT\&CK} --- was not a
formality. It was the prerequisite for everything that followed.

\section*{The Evolving Threat Landscape}

The second chapter painted the threat landscape as it stands in 2024--2025,
and the picture is sobering. APT groups are no longer just sophisticated ---
they are now \textit{AI-augmented}. Reconnaissance is automated. Phishing is
personalized at scale. Malware mutates to evade signatures. Breakout times
have collapsed. The same technology that gives defenders powerful new tools is
being handed, simultaneously, to the attackers. This duality is not a
coincidence --- it is the defining tension of modern cybersecurity, and any
detection solution that ignores it is already outdated before it is deployed.

\section*{What the Review Revealed}

The third chapter turned to solutions. A critical review of the available
datasets revealed that despite growing research activity, truly APT-specific,
well-labeled, and CKC/MITRE ATT\&CK-mapped datasets remain scarce. The
\textbf{Linux-APT-Dataset-2024}, \textbf{KAPT-2024}, and
\textbf{CICAPT-IIoT} datasets represent meaningful progress, but the field
still suffers from a lack of standardized, realistic benchmarks that reflect
the multi-stage nature of real APT campaigns.

Across the three families of solutions reviewed --- traditional Machine
Learning, Deep Learning, and Generative AI --- several conclusions emerge
clearly:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Traditional ML} methods such as Random Forests and SVMs
    remain strong baselines, particularly when interpretability and low
    computational cost are priorities. However, they struggle with the
    sequential, multi-stage nature of APT attacks.

    \item \textbf{Deep Learning} approaches --- CNNs, LSTMs, GNNs, and
    Transformer-based models --- offer substantially higher expressive power
    and are better suited to capturing temporal and structural patterns in
    attack sequences. Graph Neural Networks in particular show great promise
    for modeling the lateral movement and multi-hop behavior characteristic
    of APTs.

    \item \textbf{Generative AI} approaches --- autoencoders, VAEs, and
    Reinforcement Learning agents --- open new frontiers. They address the
    chronic problem of data imbalance, enable anomaly detection without
    requiring labeled attack samples, and allow agents to adapt dynamically
    to evolving threats. The \textbf{OGE-DQN} model reviewed in this report
    exemplifies how RL-based agents can outperform static classifiers on
    zero-day and contaminated test scenarios.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Open Challenges}

Despite this progress, important challenges remain open. No single model
today achieves both high detection coverage across the full MITRE ATT\&CK
matrix and robust generalization to unseen attack variants. The gap between
controlled benchmark performance and real-world deployment is still wide.
Explainability --- the ability to tell a security analyst \textit{why} a
model flagged an event, not just \textit{that} it did --- remains an unsolved
problem for most deep architectures. And the adversarial arms race means that
any static model, however accurate today, risks obsolescence tomorrow.

\section*{Looking Forward}

What this report ultimately reveals is that the future of APT detection lies
not in any single algorithm, but in the \textbf{combination}: structured
frameworks like CKC and MITRE ATT\&CK to provide semantic grounding,
graph-based and sequential models to capture attack progression, and
generative or adaptive components to handle novelty and imbalance. The field
is moving, steadily, toward detection systems that do not just recognize known
threats --- but reason about unknown ones.

This report was written as a stepping stone. The knowledge built here --- the
understanding of APT mechanics, the landscape of existing datasets, the
strengths and limits of each ML family --- forms the direct foundation for
the upcoming work: \textit{``GenAI-Augmented Framework for Early Detection of
Multi-Stage APT Attacks using Cyber Kill Chain''}. That work will take
everything examined in this report and push it further: designing a framework
that is not just aware of the attack lifecycle, but actively guided by it ---
using Generative AI not as an accessory, but as a core component of
detection. This report is the map. What comes next is the journey.

We hope this report served not only as a map of that landscape, but as a
readable one. If at any point an analogy made a concept click, or a warning
box saved you from a common pitfall, then we achieved what we set out to do
--- to write something useful, from one human to another.
